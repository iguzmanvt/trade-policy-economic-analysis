# Financing loss and damage response without obscuring future obligations

**Isaac Guzmán Valdivia Tovar · Independent analytical sample · 26 October 2026**

## Question and finding

How should a financing assessment distinguish immediate liquidity, the present value of repayment obligations and exposure to subsequent fiscal stress? A package can be concessional in present-value terms while leaving substantial annual repayments after its principal-payment holiday. Separately, a grant can fail to meet an immediate cash need if disbursement arrives late.

This exercise makes those distinctions through four packages with the same USD 100 million initial funding. All inputs are synthetic. The concessional-loan example has USD 63.37 million in present-value repayments at a 5% discount benchmark, but annual service rises from USD 1.00 million in year 5 to USD 7.67 million in year 6. Combining a 25% increase in the local-currency price of the dollar with a 20% smaller assumed fiscal envelope increases its peak service-to-envelope ratio from 7.67% to 11.98%. These results illustrate contractual and accounting mechanisms; they are not estimates for a country or an FRLD funding proposal.

## Institutional boundary

The FRLD Governing Instrument provides for grants and highly concessional loans on the basis of Board policy, with debt sustainability considerations, and permits a wider range of instruments. This broad mandate must be distinguished from the instruments made operational in a particular funding window. The 2026 Board report records the Barbados Implementation Modalities as grant interventions for 2025–2026. The loan comparisons below are analytical possibilities, not BIM lending terms or a recommendation that vulnerable countries borrow to respond to loss and damage. [1–2]

## Equal initial funding and different repayment profiles

Every package is fully disbursed at time 0. Interest is paid annually in arrears on opening principal; it is not capitalized. Loans use equal annual principal payments. Five principal-payment-holiday years mean no principal is paid at the ends of years 1–5, followed by 15 principal payments at the ends of years 6–20. The reference loan has 10 annual repayments beginning in year 1. There are no fees, defaults, refinancing, additional disbursements or prepayments.

| Synthetic funding package | Grant / loan, USDm | Annual interest rate | Final payment / first principal year | PV of repayments at 5%, USDm | Package PV support measure |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Grant | 100 / 0 | — | — | 0.00 | 100.00% |
| Concessional loan | 0 / 100 | 1% | 20 / 6 | 63.37 | 36.63% |
| Half grant, half concessional loan | 50 / 50 | 1% on loan | 20 / 6 | 31.69 | 68.31% |
| Reference loan | 0 / 100 | 5% | 10 / 1 | 100.00 | 0.00% |

The last column is an analytical comparison: initial funding minus discounted repayments, divided by initial funding. With initial funding F and annual debt service DS, the calculation is `100 × [F − Σ DS(t)/(1+r)^t] / F`. It is not cash available at disbursement, a budgetary subsidy booked by a lender, a measured welfare gain or an official concessionality certification. The 36.63% measure for the loan does not mean that 36.63% of its proceeds are a grant: the full USD 100 million remains contractually repayable principal.

IDA describes loan concessionality using the difference between face value and discounted debt service. The present exercise uses that general valuation logic, with an explicitly specified annual-payment convention and no fees. It does not assert replication of an official calculator or its treatment of timing and charges. The included 3%, 5% and 7% benchmarks show that the apparent support measure depends on the discount assumption. [3]

## Principal holidays and currency risk

The concessional loan requires USD 1 million of annual interest during its first five years. Its principal remains USD 100 million at the end of year 5. When equal principal payments begin, annual service reaches USD 7.67 million; the mixed package requires half this amount. The principal holiday defers obligations and reduces their present value at a positive discount rate; it does not erase principal.

![Annual repayments and fiscal-envelope sensitivity](figures/repayment_and_fiscal_stress.svg)

For a transparent sensitivity calculation, the baseline exchange rate is 1 synthetic local-currency unit per dollar and an annual nominal budget envelope is 100 million local units. From year 6, the currency scenario raises the local-currency price of USD by 25%; the combined scenario also lowers the envelope to 80 million. Contractual USD repayments are unchanged.

| Package | Baseline peak service / envelope | With currency shock | With currency and envelope shocks |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Grant | 0.00% | 0.00% | 0.00% |
| Concessional loan | 7.67% | 9.58% | 11.98% |
| Half grant, half concessional loan | 3.83% | 4.79% | 5.99% |
| Reference loan | 15.00% | 15.63% | 19.53% |

The numerator includes only service on the new loan; the denominator is a chosen envelope, not observed government revenue or a prudential limit. Peak years can differ: the reference loan peaks in year 1 under baseline assumptions and in year 6 under the combined shock. Ratios cannot establish an acceptable debt burden. A country assessment would require the existing debt portfolio and broader macro-fiscal projections. The World Bank describes the LIC Debt Sustainability Framework as combining baseline projections, stress tests, country debt-carrying capacity and judgment; this exercise implements none of its country classification or risk-rating machinery. [4]

## Liquidity depends on when cash arrives

A separate monthly illustration holds funding at USD 100 million and an opening cash buffer at USD 10 million. Spending needs are 40, 30, 20 and 10 million in months 1–4. Receipts occur at the start of the designated month, before that month's spending. This experiment does not change the annual loan schedules or their present values.

| Month in which all funding is received | Peak temporary cash shortfall, USDm | Months with a shortfall |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0 | 0 |
| 3 | 60 | 2 |
| 6 | 90 | 5 |

The shortfall is the maximum of cumulative spending less cumulative receipts and the opening buffer, bounded below by zero. It is a potential need for bridging resources or expenditure rescheduling. Summing monthly shortfalls would count an unresolved gap repeatedly. The exercise neither assumes bridge borrowing is feasible nor prices its cost.

## What a funding review would need next

An operational recommendation should connect the verified spending profile to disbursement conditions, the available cash buffer and the consequences of delay. Where debt is considered, the review should document the complete term sheet, currency exposure, existing obligations, contingent liabilities and how repayment fits the applicable country debt assessment. It should also establish the legal borrower and recipient, approval authority, control of funds, reporting requirements, procurement and assurance arrangements, and the evidence for the proposed loss-and-damage response.

Direct budget support describes a route and modality of delivery; it does not, by itself, specify whether the underlying funding is a grant or a repayable instrument. Choosing a nominally attractive loan cannot substitute for fiduciary assessment or determine functional equivalency. A grant's absence of contractual repayment also does not establish delivery effectiveness. The four packages consume different provider resources and are therefore not alternatives with an equal fiscal cost to the funder. No optimal allocation or welfare ranking is inferred.

This model examines financing cash flows. It does not value non-economic losses, attribute a disaster to climate change, estimate mitigation or adaptation benefits, design insurance premiums or guarantees, or certify that an activity falls within a funding window. Those questions require separate evidence and specialist review.
