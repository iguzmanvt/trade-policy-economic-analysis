# From textual trade concerns to an inspectable evidence base

Isaac Guzmán Valdivia Tovar | 22 October 2026

## A documentation problem with policy consequences

A trade-barrier report combines different objects: descriptions of regulations, complaints about implementation, references to negotiations and statements about measures that preserve access. Treating each mention as a new barrier would lose these distinctions. This example asks how to build a small, inspectable record of non-tariff measures affecting cheese while preserving who reports the concern, where it applies and what the text can support.

The sample contains 18 edition–issue records from the Office of the United States Trade Representative (USTR) National Trade Estimate (NTE) reports for 2024–2026. Eight issue identifiers connect repeated records across seven country/regional chapters. The released coding is a new public reconstruction within an earlier research window of 2007–2026. It is a purposive sample chosen to demonstrate different mechanisms and temporal interpretation. It is not an inventory of all cheese mentions or a ranking of countries.

The underlying mechanisms differ. The Canada entries concern permitted dry milk protein use in cheese manufacture. The Dominican Republic entries concern reported internal tax treatment. Mexico supplies examples of proposed conformity assessment and of geographical indication (GI) concerns accompanied by an access assurance. Qatar's white-cheese shelf-life issue appears within the Gulf Cooperation Council chapter. The European Union (EU) entries cite Danbo and Havarti; Chile and Vietnam illustrate prior-user or market-access arrangements. See the row-level printed and PDF page references in `data/nte_cheese_sample.csv`.[1–3]

This separation guides further analysis. Ingredient restrictions call for production and input-substitution evidence. Conformity procedures call for information on testing, certification, delays and recognition. Tax differences require the applicable legal treatment and transaction bases. Name protection requires product-specific rights, eligible users and the exact commercial presentation. Shelf-life requirements require product characteristics, logistics and actual enforcement. A single frequency measure cannot substitute for these distinct evidence needs.

## Temporal case: a changed reference is not a new barrier

The EU records carry the same issue identifier across all three editions. The 2024 NTE discussion cites Regulation (EU) 1151/2012; the 2025 and 2026 discussions cite Regulation (EU) 2024/1143. All three retain concern about GI protection and cheese names. The output `results/temporal_cases.csv` preserves the reported instrument and page reference for each edition.[1–3]

The observable result is continuity of a reported concern alongside a change in the cited instrument. This is evidence about the report's content. It does not establish that the new instrument created a new restriction, increased its severity or changed actual access for a specific exporter. Those propositions require the legal text, transition provisions and implementation evidence. Maintaining an issue identifier prevents counting the same concern three times as three distinct measures.

Chile offers a complementary coding lesson. Its 2025 entry records a market-access/prior-user arrangement and its 2026 entry again describes a side letter addressing prior users. The sample records these as arrangements, rather than automatically treating any paragraph containing cheese and GI language as a newly imposed barrier.[2–3]

## What a country team can use

The immediate product is a traceable evidence table suitable for checking exposure or framing a focused legal and economic inquiry. It preserves source edition, jurisdiction, mechanism, status, original summary and page locator. Every selected passage was read in context; optional code verifies official-PDF hashes and the presence of page anchors. A reviewer can return to a specific claim without relying on a search snippet or a count of keywords.

For surveillance or policy dialogue, a useful next question would connect a specific measure to affected trade, eligible firms, administrative costs or observed market access. The current sample does not supply those quantities and does not estimate an ad valorem equivalent, welfare effect or growth impact. It also does not independently adjudicate whether a measure violates a World Trade Organization obligation. USTR's viewpoint is retained as the source's viewpoint.

## Scope discipline

Twelve records are classified as ongoing concerns. The remaining six retain an assurance, an arrangement or prior-user monitoring. These are classifications of selected passages, not mutually comparable measures of restrictiveness. Repetition can reflect persistence, reporting practice or emphasis. An issue omitted from this sample is unobserved here; omission does not show that a measure disappeared. A report edition is also distinct from the adoption date of a measure.

The reproducible outputs therefore support documentation and temporal reading. The broader 20-edition corpus and economic-cost estimation are outside this release. The bounded sample is complete for the records declared here; no claim of exhaustive coverage is made.
